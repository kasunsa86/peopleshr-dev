A Guide to Employee Retention in 2026: How to Make Your Top Talent Stay

Updated October 2026 · 9 min read

Do you feel like you’re in a constant struggle to keep your best people? You’re not alone, and in 2026 the struggle looks different from a few years ago.

The Great Resignation is over. Quit rates have cooled, and many employees are staying put because the job market feels uncertain. But staying is not the same as being committed. Gallup’s State of the Global Workplace 2026 report found that only 20% of employees worldwide were engaged at work in 2025, the lowest level since 2020 and the first time engagement has fallen two years in a row. No region improved.

That gap between “still here” and “all in” is where retention is won or lost. When the market opens up, disengaged people leave first, and they’re rarely the ones you’d choose to lose.

The good news: we now have better data than ever on why people stay, and the tools to act on it before someone hands in their notice. Here’s how to use both.

Retaining employees versus replacing them

Replacing an employee costs between half and twice their annual salary, according to Gallup. For a 100-person company with an average salary of $50,000, Gallup estimates that adds up to roughly $660,000 to $2.6 million a year in turnover costs.

The salary is only the visible part. The full bill includes:

  • Direct costs: job ads, recruiter or agency fees, interview hours, background checks and onboarding.
  • Ramp-up time: a new hire can take months to reach the output of the person they replaced.
  • Lost knowledge: client relationships, process shortcuts and context that were never written down.
  • Team strain: remaining colleagues absorb the workload, which raises their own risk of burnout and exit.

The most important number, though, is how much of this is avoidable. Work Institute’s 2025 Retention Report, based on more than 120,000 exit interviews, found that 75% of employee departures were preventable. And Gallup found that 52% of people who quit say their manager or organisation could have done something to keep them. In 51% of cases, nobody had spoken to them about their satisfaction or future in the three months before they left.

In other words, most turnover isn’t bad luck. It’s a missed conversation.

The hidden benefits of employee retention

The biggest payoff from retention isn’t the recruitment budget you save. It’s keeping the people who move your business forward.

  • Your best ideas stay in-house. Long-tenured top performers carry the customer insight and process knowledge that drives innovation. When they leave, that edge often walks straight to a competitor.
  • Morale compounds. When people see strong colleagues choosing to stay and grow, it signals that this is a place worth building a career in.
  • HR gets its time back. Every resignation pulls HR into a cycle of backfilling, screening and onboarding. Lower turnover frees that time for development, engagement and workforce planning.
  • Productivity stays steady. Exits delay projects and push extra work onto the team. Overloaded colleagues become the next flight risk, and the cycle repeats.
  • AI adoption sticks. In 2026 this one matters more than ever. Gallup describes engagement as a measure of readiness for change: organisations with engaged employees tend to handle disruption, including AI, more successfully. Constant churn means constantly re-training people on the systems you just rolled out.

What makes employees stay in 2026?

People stay for fair pay, visible growth and a manager who invests in them. Everything else builds on those three.

In Southeast Asia, the stakes are high. Aon’s 2026 Salary Increase and Turnover Study found attrition is still in double digits in every market it covers, averaging 16.6% across the region.

1. Fair, transparent pay

Pay is back at the top of the list. McKinsey’s HR Monitor 2026, as reported by Becker’s Hospital Review, found compensation jumped from fifth to first as the reason employees stay, cited by more than half of the 5,500 employees surveyed. Yet 57% said they had no salary increase in the past 12 months.

You don’t have to outbid every competitor. But people need to trust that pay is fair, consistent and explained. Benchmark regularly, close unexplained gaps, and make sure payroll is accurate every single cycle. A wrong payslip erodes trust faster than almost anything else.

2. A visible path to grow

Career was the number one reason people left their jobs in Q2 2026, behind 19.6% of exits in Work Institute’s Quarterly Workforce Trends Report. The top drivers were wanting a different career path and a lack of promotion opportunities.

Many organisations underestimate the gap. In McKinsey’s survey, one in five employees had no career development conversation or feedback session in the past year, while HR estimated that figure at just 3%.

To make growth real:

  • Advertise roles internally before going external, and make it easy to apply.
  • Map skills, not just job titles, so you can spot who is ready for a stretch move.
  • Hold career conversations at least quarterly, not once a year at appraisal time.
  • Offer lateral moves and project rotations when promotions are scarce.

3. Managers who coach, not just manage

Managers are under more pressure than anyone. Gallup found global manager engagement fell from 31% in 2022 to 22% in 2025, and that drop explains most of the overall decline in engagement. Flatter structures and bigger teams are part of the cause.

It isn’t inevitable. In Gallup’s best-practice organisations, 79% of managers are engaged. The difference is investment: training managers to coach, keeping spans of control realistic, and giving them the data to see how their team is really doing.

4. Flexibility and work-life balance

Work-life balance (46%) and flexibility (33%) are the second and fourth most-cited reasons to stay in McKinsey’s survey. For shift-based and frontline teams, flexibility looks different: Work Institute found shift and schedule concerns drove 7.9% of all exits in Q2 2026.

Fair rostering, predictable schedules, easy shift swaps and self-service leave all count as flexibility, even when remote work isn’t an option.

5. Recognition and respect

People leave when their effort goes unnoticed or when they don’t feel respected. Recognition doesn’t need to be expensive. It needs to be frequent, specific and visible: a manager calling out good work in a team meeting, peer-to-peer shout-outs, and celebrating milestones like work anniversaries and certifications.

6. AI skills and modern tools

This is the newest retention driver, and it cuts both ways. Gallup found 18% of U.S. employees now think their job is likely to be eliminated by technology within five years. Uncertainty pushes people to look elsewhere.

The antidote is investment. When employees strongly agree their manager actively supports their team’s use of AI, they are 7.4 times as likely to say AI gives them more chances to do what they do best. Yet McKinsey found only about 30% of U.S. employees received formal generative AI training. Upskilling is quickly becoming one of the clearest signals that an employer sees a future for you.

Modern tools matter for everyday work too. Clunky systems, paper-based leave and hard-to-find payslips are small frustrations that add up.

7. Wellbeing and purpose

Forty percent of employees worldwide reported a lot of stress the previous day, according to Gallup. Wellbeing improves when people see their work as rewarding and useful to others, and when they feel they have choices in what they do. Connect each role to the outcome it serves, and treat workload and burnout as business risks, not personal problems.

8. A strong first year

Employees with less than one year of service made up 27% of all separations in Work Institute’s Q2 2026 data. Retention starts on day one. A structured onboarding plan, a named buddy, clear 30-60-90 day goals and early check-ins lower the chance of a new hire walking out.

Spot flight risk before the resignation letter

The best time to retain someone is months before they decide to leave. By the time a resignation lands, the decision is usually made.

The warning signs are often already in your HR data. You just need to connect them:

  • Engagement dips: falling survey scores or silence where someone used to give feedback.
  • Stalled growth: no promotion, pay review or role change in a long time, especially for high performers.
  • Attendance shifts: more short-notice leave, more absences or unusual overtime patterns.
  • Workload strain: a team carrying extra load after a colleague left.
  • Manager changes: a new manager or a team with consistently low engagement.

Looking at each of these in a separate spreadsheet makes the pattern almost impossible to see. That’s where people analytics comes in.

Your 2026 employee retention checklist

Use this list to check where your retention strategy stands today.

  • We know our voluntary turnover rate by team, role and tenure, not just company-wide.
  • We track regrettable turnover (high performers) separately.
  • Pay is benchmarked at least once a year and pay decisions can be explained.
  • Payroll runs accurately and on time every cycle.
  • Open roles are advertised internally first.

Frequently asked questions

What is employee retention?

Employee retention is an organisation’s ability to keep its employees over time. It’s usually measured as the percentage of employees who stay over a set period, such as a year.

How do you calculate employee retention rate?

Divide the number of employees who stayed for the whole period by the number of employees at the start of the period, then multiply by 100. Exclude new hires who joined during the period.

What is a good employee retention rate in 2026?

It varies by industry and market. As a reference point, Aon found average attrition of 16.6% across Southeast Asia in 2026, which means roughly 83% retention. Compare yourself against your own industry and country, and watch your trend over time.

What is the number one reason employees leave?

Career growth. It was the top reason for leaving in Work Institute’s Q2 2026 exit data, followed by health and family, and work-life balance. Pay has risen sharply as a reason to stay, ranking first in McKinsey’s HR Monitor 2026.

How much does employee turnover cost?

Gallup estimates that replacing an employee costs between half and twice their annual salary, depending on the role.

How can AI help with employee retention?

AI-powered people analytics can combine engagement, attendance, pay and performance data to flag flight risk early. Tools like Lexi let HR leaders ask questions in plain language and see which teams need attention before people resign.

Make retention a strategy, not a reaction

In 2026, a quiet job market is hiding a lot of disengagement. Organisations that use this period to fix pay fairness, build career paths, support their managers and invest in skills will keep their best people when the market opens up again.

The common thread is visibility. You can’t fix what you can’t see, and you can’t afford to find out someone was unhappy only at their exit interview.
